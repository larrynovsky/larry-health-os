# Diagnostic Hypothesis

Once medical students have developed an understanding of clinical vocabulary and can conceptualize patient problems using semantic qualifiers [[ch03_08_system 1 and 2 thinking as dual processes]], teachers introduce the concept of illness scripts (Illness script) to help them consider features of a typical diagnosis. Illness scripts are mental representations of coherent, causal clinical knowledge used in clinical reasoning.

Custers summarizes the common components of illness scripts as follows:

1. High-level, precompiled conceptual knowledge structures stored in long-term memory.
2. Represent general event sequences interconnected by temporal and often causal or hierarchical relationships.
3. Can be activated as integral wholes in appropriate contexts.
4. Contain variables and slots that can be filled with information from the actual situation, retrieved from memory, or inferred from context.
5. Develop through direct or vicarious clinical experience.

While early medical students may not have enough experience to fully develop their own illness scripts, they begin forming rudimentary versions as they learn about typical presentations of clinical cases. Illness scripts help integrate foundational science concepts and pathophysiology with clinical history, physical examination findings, and diagnostic testing results.

**Example:**
A 28-year-old male presents to the emergency room after collapsing during his first soccer match. His friends report a loss of consciousness for about 30 seconds. He has experienced mild shortness of breath when exercising since he can remember but now finds it significantly worse, with exercise tolerance declining over the past nine months.

Initial problem representation: Young active male with sudden collapse and syncope.

Triggered diagnostic hypotheses:
- Cardiac etiology
- Neurological etiology
- Intravascular volume loss from trauma
- Pulmonary embolism

Hypothesis-driven inquiry:
Did he lose consciousness? Was there any involuntary motor activity?
Was there any postictal symptom?

As the history and physical examination findings are integrated, more specific diagnoses can be hypothesized, such as aortic stenosis or hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy. The summary statement integrates all these elements into a concise diagnosis.

For example:
Summary Statement: A 28-year-old male with sudden-onset syncope, moderate to severe dyspnea with orthopnea, intermittent chest pain typical of angina, progressive fatigue, and an unclear history of childhood heart murmur. Exam suggests heart failure, murmurs consistent with aortic stenosis.

An echocardiogram would be the best approach for distinguishing between diagnoses like aortic stenosis, hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy, or idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension [[ch04_06_hypothesis driven inquiry]].

**[[Illness Script]] Worksheet** (Table 4.4):
This worksheet provides structure to build knowledge storage in an illness script framework, including categories like enabling conditions, pathophysiological fault, and clinical consequences.

[[Enabling Conditions]]:
- Age, sex, race, ethnicity
- Family history, genetics, habits, exposures, medications, nested comorbidities

Pathophysiological [[Fault]]: Mechanisms of insult or injury (e.g., hemodynamic regulation, genetic mutation)

Clinical Consequences:
- Onset, site, severity, chronology
- Physical exam findings, laboratory findings, imaging findings, diagnostic procedure findings

Building an illness script mental repository in long-term memory requires extensive case elaboration with guidance and reflection on clinical cases or simulations [[ch09_03_the process of curriculum development]].
