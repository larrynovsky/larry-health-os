In a 1996 review, Custers et al.ch03_04_knowledge-representations-to-support-reasoning categorized the thinking about how physicians' cognition is organized around clinical knowledge into three alternative frameworks: prototypes, instances, and semantic networks. Each of these models has its strengths and weaknesses in explaining clinical reasoning.

**Semantic Network Theory** posits the existence of nodes representing units of information connected with other nodes within a network. The strength of each node and the overall network depends on how frequently it is used. This theory suggests that medical knowledge can be represented as interconnected nodes, where **schemas** and **illness scripts**[[ch03_13_neuroscience and visual expertise in clinical reasoning]] are meaningful connections between these units. These schemas and illness scripts can be strengthened or adapted based on a clinician's experience.

This model contrasts with the prototype framework, which suggests that repeated encounters with similar diseases lead to remembering common denominators resulting in single prototypes in long-term memory. It also differs from the instances framework, which posits that physicians remember individual patient encounters without abstraction and retain context-specific information.
