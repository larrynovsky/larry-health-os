Custers recently summarized **illness script** theory as high-level conceptual knowledge structures in long-term memory, representing general event sequences with interconnected individual events by temporal and often causal or hierarchical relationships. Illness scripts are activated as integral wholes in appropriate clinical contexts containing relevant variables such as patient findings [[ch03_12_bias and error in clinical reasoning]].

**Illness script** theory was first introduced by Barrows and Feltovich, who described them as chunks of long-term memory comprising three components: enabling conditions (past history and causes), fault (pathophysiology), and consequences (signs and symptoms) [[ch03_01_concepts and definitions]]. The theory has since been elaborated further by Schmidt and Boshuizen.

Illness scripts are stored in long-term memory as units with temporal components, similar to a film script of unfolding events. Patients are remembered as instances of these scripts, and physicians build larger repertoires of illness scripts through experience, continually refining them over time [[ch03_07_encapsulation of knowledge and the intermediate effect]].

When an experienced physician initially sees a patient, verbal and nonverbal information is thought to immediately activate relevant **illness script**s. This effortless, fast thinking process, or nonanalytic reasoning, is referred to as script activation [[ch02_06_artificial intelligence and problem based learning]]. In some cases, only one script is activated leading to a correct diagnosis (e.g., “type II diabetes mellitus”). In other cases, multiple scripts are activated requiring comparison and contrast through analytic or slow thinking. Early learners may not activate any scripts upon first encounter with a patient, whereas experts may activate one or several illness scripts.
