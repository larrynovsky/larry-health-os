In a 1996 review, Custers and colleagues categorized the thinking about the way physician’s cognition is organized around clinical knowledge into three alternative frameworks: prototypes, instances, or semantic networks ch03_04_knowledge-representations-to-support-reasoning. These mental representations have assets and drawbacks in their explanatory power for clinical reasoning.

**Prototype Framework:**
The prototype framework or **prototype theory** assumes that multiple encounters with related diseases lead physicians to remember the common denominators, resulting in single prototypes in long-term memory [[ch03_13_neuroscience and visual expertise in clinical reasoning]]. Prototypes represent typical cases and are used as a reference point for diagnosing similar cases.

**[[Instances Framework]]:**
The instances framework assumes that physicians actually remember individual patient encounters without abstraction. Context-specific information may be part of these instances, making each encounter unique [[ch02_06_artificial intelligence and problem based learning]]. This approach emphasizes the importance of specific clinical experiences over generalized knowledge.

**[[Semantic Network Theory]]:**
The semantic network theory posits the existence of nodes of information units connected with other nodes in a network. The strength of the network and its nodes depends on the intensity of their use [[ch03_09_case specificity and context specificity]]. Schemas and **illness scripts**, medically meaningful interconnected nodes, can be strengthened and adapted based on clinical experience [[ch02_04_william osler and the differential diagnosis]].

Each framework offers a different perspective on how medical knowledge is organized and used in reasoning. Prototype theory focuses on typical cases that serve as templates for future diagnoses, while the instances approach highlights the importance of unique patient encounters. Semantic networks provide a more structured view of interconnected medical knowledge units.
