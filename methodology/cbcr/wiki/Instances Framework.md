In a 1996 review, Custers and colleagues categorized the thinking about the way physician's cognition is organized around clinical knowledge into three alternative frameworks ch03_04_knowledge-representations-to-support-reasoning. These mental representations could take the form of prototypes, instances, or semantic networks. Each model has its own strengths and limitations in explaining clinical reasoning.

The **instances framework** assumes that physicians remember individual instances of patient encounters without abstraction ch03_04_knowledge-representations-to-support-reasoning. This means that context-specific information is part of these instances, making them highly detailed and specific to the situation encountered. Unlike prototypes which generalize common features across cases, the instances framework emphasizes unique aspects of each encounter.

This approach contrasts with **prototype theory** which posits that multiple encounters with related diseases lead physicians to remember common denominators, resulting in single generalized prototypes stored in long-term memory ch03_04_knowledge-representations-to-support-reasoning.

In comparison, the **semantic network theory** suggests a network of interconnected nodes where information units are linked together. The strength and adaptability of these networks depend on their frequency of use, allowing for flexible and dynamic knowledge representation ch03_04_knowledge-representations-to-support-reasoning.

While each framework offers insights into clinical reasoning, the instances framework highlights the importance of detailed, context-specific information in guiding medical decisions.
