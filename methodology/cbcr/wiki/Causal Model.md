A causal model is a simplified representation of the relationships between elements in a system, often used to explain signs and symptoms in clinical settings. In the context of [[Case-Based Clinical Reasoning]] (CBCR) education, experienced clinicians tend to encapsulate their pathophysiological knowledge into diagnostic labels or high-level causal models [[illness script]]. These simplified mental frameworks help them quickly diagnose patients by recognizing patterns and associations between symptoms and underlying diseases.

However, this process can also lead to a decrease in the ability to recall specific details about clinical cases as one becomes more experienced. This phenomenon is known as the intermediate effect, where novice clinicians sometimes outperform experts in recalling detailed case information [[intermediate effect]].

In CBCR sessions, students are encouraged to verbalize their thought processes and fill out tables that justify how signs and symptoms confirm or disclaim particular hypotheses about a patient's condition. This practice helps them develop the ability to articulate their reasoning, even when it becomes more routine and encapsulated over time student role.
