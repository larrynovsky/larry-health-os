### The Aim of [[Faculty Development]]
Most faculty members of medical schools and universities have been trained primarily as clinicians or scientists, with few having specific training in teaching. However, as education becomes more sophisticated, there is a growing recognition that effective teaching skills must be explicitly developed. Utrecht University Medical Center (UMC) exemplifies this approach through its tiered model of teaching certificates for faculty members, ranging from basic to advanced levels.

Table 9.3 outlines the certification process:
- **Student [[Teaching Certificate]]:** Optional for senior medical students interested in a teaching rotation.
- **Teaching Certificate:** Required for all faculty members.
- **Advanced Teaching Certificate:** Required for senior faculty in leadership roles.
- **Postgraduate Scholarly Educator Certificate:** Optional for those aspiring to a career in educational scholarship.
- **PhD Diploma in Health Professions Education Research:** For educators aiming to become researchers of education in the health professions.

Medical educators worldwide now advocate that faculty must be trained before they can teach, akin to how students are not permitted to treat patients without proper training. Universities increasingly require new faculty to obtain a basic teaching certificate and advanced certification for leadership positions.

Molenaar et al.'s (2009) framework outlines detailed teaching competencies across domains such as development, organization, execution, coaching, assessment, and program evaluation, with varying levels of responsibility based on macro-meso-micro distinctions. This serves as an excellent foundation for faculty development in medical education.

### Evidence of Effectiveness
CBCR courses have been well-received over decades due to high student ratings and perceived value by students and schools alike. While direct comparative studies are limited, a notable study by Schmidt et al. (1996) found superior performance among Amsterdam students who had undergone CBCR training compared to other universities during preclinical years 2 and 3. Additionally, recent research by Keemink et al. (n.d.) indicates that preclinical students better develop illness scripts through the CBCR course than with common cases not covered in the curriculum.

### Faculty Development for CBCR
Effective faculty development is crucial for implementing a successful CBCR program. The recommended components include:
1. **Written Instructions:** Detailed guidelines about case-based clinical reasoning education, such as this book.
2. **Training of Case Writers:** Workshops where case writers draft cases, present them to colleagues, and receive feedback over several weeks with final editing by a coordinator.
3. **Training of Consultants:** Methods include observing CBCR sessions or using instructional films showcasing the process from a student perspective.
4. **Training of Students:** Essential for those who must act as peer teachers in small-group discussions, focusing on developing new mindsets and correcting mistakes through open dialogue.

A case study from Tbilisi State Medical University (TSMU) in Georgia demonstrates successful implementation: the introduction of CBCR involved workshops, training sessions, pilot courses, formal adoption by the Academic Council, and further spread to other universities. Feedback revealed areas for improvement such as balancing student activity with consultant presence and avoiding premature disclosure of correct diagnoses.

### Alignment with Actual Cases Discussed
The Utrecht CBCR test closely follows cases discussed during CBCR sessions to reinforce learned scripts without expecting substantial transferability early in the curriculum. For senior students, deviating from discussed cases may be recommended for broader testing.

### The Process of [[Curriculum Development]]
Curriculum development involves following Kern's six steps: problem identification, needs assessment, goal setting, educational strategies selection, implementation planning, and evaluation. These steps ensure a well-rounded approach to modernizing medical curricula.

#### Table 9.1 Elements of a Curriculum Description:
- **Mission Statement:** Rationale for the curriculum’s overall intention.
- **Objectives:** Specific learner goals aligned with societal needs.
- **Intended Learners and Admission Policy:** Criteria for attracting desired students.
- **Educational Philosophy:** Grounding decisions in educational theory and methods.
- **Curriculum Framework:** Visual representation of the curriculum structure.
- **Individual Units:** Detailed descriptions of each unit including objectives, teaching methods, and assessments.
- **Methods of Assessment:** Approaches to measure learning outcomes effectively.
- **Governance:** Structure for collaboration, integration, quality control.
- **Funding and Facilities:** Adequate resources for high-quality education.
- **[[Quality Assurance]] and Faculty Development:** Continuous monitoring and improvement processes.

### [[Bedside Teaching]] and Patient Demonstration
Until the 17th century, medical teaching was theoretical. The introduction of bedside teaching by Batista de Monte in Padua (1543) marked a shift towards empirical reasoning. Herman Boerhaave’s (1668-1738) teachings at Leyden University were influential but limited due to patient access issues. Dr. Thomas Bond's 1766 lectures in Pennsylvania introduced clinical reasoning with empirical elements, setting a precedent for modern medical education.

### CBCR as an Approach to Ignite [[Curriculum Modernization]]
Since 2005, the method of [[Case-Based Clinical Reasoning]] (CBCR) has been used to reform undergraduate medical curricula in countries like Moldova, Georgia, Ukraine, and Azerbaijan. It integrates well into lecture-based systems while promoting student-centered learning and problem-solving skills.
