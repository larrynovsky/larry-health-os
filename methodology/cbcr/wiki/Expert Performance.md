Despite the finding that clinical reasoning is content-dependent and context-dependent, expertise in diagnostic and therapeutic reasoning varies among physicians with similar experience. Some internists are considered better diagnosticians and some surgeons better operators than others [[ch03_12_bias and error in clinical reasoning]]. It remains useful to think of what leads to superb performance, as education can be a part of it (Asch et al. 2014). Indeed, many scholars prefer the term expert performance when referring to clinical reasoning as it acknowledges the many nuances to this ability [[ch03_08_system 1 and 2 thinking as dual processes]] [[ch03_03_deconstructing the reasoning process]].

For procedural performance, repetitive practice is key. Competence in colonoscopy requires experience with 150–200 supervised procedures (Ekkelenkamp et al. 2016). That competence improves with practice is not surprising and known from other fields like chess (De Groot 1978). Anecdotally, in the 1960s the Hungarian educational psychologist László Polgár trained his three daughters intensively from a young age to become highly skilled chess players. Psychologist Ericsson generalized the idea that deliberate practice is key to expert performance rather than innate talent (Ericsson et al. 1993). He distinguishes three subsequent mental representations: planning phase with clear performance goals, translation to execution, and monitoring representation [[ch06_07_the peer teacher role]] [[ch04_05_contrastive learning]]. Applications in medical training have been described (Ericsson 2015) but mainly focus on procedures. Clinical reasoning may also benefit from deliberate practice [[ch03_01_concepts and definitions]], and the work of Mamede et al. using deliberate practice shows how reasoning can improve as well (Mamede et al. 2014).
