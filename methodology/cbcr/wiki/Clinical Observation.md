# [[Clinical Reasoning]] in the Hippocratean Era
Through all ages, humans have tried to make sense of complaints, symptoms, and diseases; thus, clinical reasoning is as old as humanity. In the pre-Hippocratic era, people relied on priests or authoritative individuals who had privileged access to divine intentions, interpreting diseases as messages from above. Similarly, moral terms were used to explain disease causes and symptoms as punishments.

Hippocrates (± 460 BC – ± 370 BC) acknowledged the natural nature of diseases, explaining them in terms of a disturbance of the four humors: yellow bile, black bile, blood, and phlegm. Though lacking empirical evidence, this theory remained unchallenged for two millennia due to its convincing nature.

Hippocrates emphasized careful observation and recording of all visible symptoms, bodily fluids, excretions, environmental factors, diet, and living habits. He summarized practical knowledge in aphorisms that often followed the format “if… then,” serving as a rudimentary form of clinical reasoning. These heuristics were primarily based on experience rather than logical derivation from humoral theory.

In today’s clinical practice, such heuristics still play an important role in diagnosis, though they are generally supported by knowledge of underlying biomedical and pathophysiological mechanisms ch03_12_bias-and-error-in-clinical-reasoning.
## Abraham Flexner and the Science of Clinical Medicine
Abraham Flexner (1865–1959) was a pioneer in American medical education, advocating for an encompassing view on how clinical medicine should be taught. He emphasized three formats: individual patient observation, case demonstration by instructors, and exposition of principles.
Flexner saw the teaching clinic as a laboratory similar to basic sciences but lagging behind in scientific rigor. He believed that diagnostic problem solving could benefit from a scientific approach akin to physiology, pathology, and biochemistry.
However, Flexner’s views were met with ambivalence: while abstract support for a scientific approach was found among physicians and teachers, concrete implementation faced resistance due to practices such as “intelligent guesswork” and improvised therapy relying on the physician's authority.

Half a century later, Becker et al. (1961) observed that teaching in clinics remained haphazard and lacked systematic approaches ch02_05_abraham-flexner-and-the-science-of-clinical-medicine.
## Rooms Arrangement and Facilities for CBCR Sessions
Suitable rooms should allow students to face each other and their peer teachers. A square table arrangement with 2–4 chairs on each side is recommended, with peer teachers sitting next to each other and the consultant at a corner.
A board (blackboard or whiteboard) is necessary to build clinical reasoning through interactive dialogues about diagnostic hypotheses, history, signs, symptoms, test results, etc. Increasingly, computer projection screens are used for teaching purposes ch06_04_rooms-arrangement-and-facilities.
## The [[Consultant]] Role
The consultant oversees CBCR sessions, assesses students’ participation, and provides feedback to both students and peer teachers. They also distribute materials for the next session.
The most important role of the consultant is to guide sessions adaptively based on the quality of guidance provided by peer teachers, asking clarifying questions or offering perspectives when necessary ch06_08_the-consultant-role.
## Summary of CBCR Method
CBCR focuses on practicing clinical reasoning in small groups. A course consists of a series of group sessions over a prolonged period (semester, year, or years). Students meet regularly (every 3–4 weeks) in fixed groups of 10–12 students.
The case vignettes reflect the patient's presentation at the clinician’s office and include questions and assignments. New findings about the patient are provided during the session by a facilitator ch01_02_summary-of-the-cbcr-method.
## The Student Role
Students must have read about the topic in advance and prepared initial general questions of the case. They should realize that verbalization of thoughts is essential for acquiring clinical reasoning skills, even if these become routine over time.
During sessions, students fill out typical tables justifying how signs and symptoms confirm or disclaim particular hypotheses ch06_06_the-student-role.
