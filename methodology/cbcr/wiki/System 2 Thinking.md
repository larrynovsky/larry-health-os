Dual process theory refers to two processes that are thought to apply during reasoning ch03_08_system-1-and-2-thinking-as-dual-processes. Briefly, dual process theory argues that we have two general thought processes: fast thinking (System I thinking or 'nonanalytic' reasoning) and slow or analytic thinking (System 2 thinking). Fast thinking is quick, subconscious, and typically effortless. An example of a fast thinking strategy is pattern recognition ch03_12_bias-and-error-in-clinical-reasoning, such as when a physician recognizes the cardinal features of Graves’ disease in a patient presenting with palpitations, exophthalmia, fine resting tremor, and thyromegaly. Slow or analytic thinking, on the other hand, is effortful and conscious. An example would be working through a patient’s acid-base status by calculating an anion gap and using Winter's formula ch05_02_current-methods-of-assessing-clinical-reasoning.

Dual process theory has been popularized in the book 'Thinking, Fast and Slow' by Daniel Kahneman (2011). Recent work argues that both processes are used simultaneously. Efficient clinical work requires fast thinking; otherwise, the capacity of working memory would be overloaded if analytic reasoning were required for all decisions ch06_04_rooms-arrangement-and-facilities.

System 1 and System 2 can be viewed as a continuum (Custers 2013). In clinical practice, rapid System 1 thinking prevails but is not infallible. Slowing down to use System 2 thinking when System 1 fails may or may not lead to more accurate decisions ch06_08_the-consultant-role. For instance, inexperienced learners tend to rely on rule-based reasoning (System 2) in complex cases, while experienced clinicians rely on case recall from memory (System 1).

Teaching clinical reasoning involves focusing on things that can go wrong and threats to effective thinking. Common errors include faulty knowledge, data gathering issues, test result interpretation problems, lack of diagnostic verification, anchoring bias, affective bias, availability bias, and context bias ch03_02_early-thinking-of-clinical-reasoning-the-computer-analogy. Errors in clinical reasoning pertain to both System 1 and System 2 thinking. Tailored teaching strategies are recommended for clinical reasoning, such as building a framework for differential diagnosis using anatomy, pathology, and semantic qualifiers (age, gender, ethnicity, main complaint) ch08_04_peer-teacher-version.

Teachers often teach students to analyze pathophysiologically because junior students lack an extensive knowledge base. However, this approach resembles the 'do as I say, not as I do' method and is less effective than whole-case approaches, where all information is presented at once ch09_06_faculty-development-for-cbcr. Whole-case approaches support clinical reasoning education better by avoiding a separation of biomedical knowledge acquisition from clinical training.

Training students in clinical reasoning is challenging. Students need extensive experience to build illness scripts and acquire a large knowledge base before they can think like doctors using System 1 pattern recognition ch03_09_case-specificity-and-context-specificity. Simulated clinical encounters can help, but both a basic science knowledge base and mental illness script repository must be available for System 2 reasoning as well.
