## Representative [[Bias]]

In clinical reasoning, representative bias occurs when clinicians rely solely on signs and symptoms to form suspicions about diagnoses, disregarding the prevalence of competing diagnoses. This bias can lead to overconfidence in certain diagnoses based on superficial similarities rather than comprehensive evaluation.

According to Kempainen et al. (2003), representative bias is one among several biases that impact clinical reasoning. Other notable biases include availability bias, confirmation bias, anchoring bias, bounded rationality bias, and outcome bias. Each of these biases contributes to errors in diagnosis and treatment planning by influencing the way clinicians interpret data and make decisions.

It's important to note that while these biases are often seen as negative influences on clinical reasoning, they can also serve heuristic purposes in guiding efficient decision-making processes (Gigerenzer and Gaissmaier 2011; Gigerenzer 2007). However, when focusing on reducing diagnostic errors, interventions aimed at identifying heuristics and biases have shown limited success (Norman et al. 2017).

In the context of [[Case-Based Clinical Reasoning]] (CBCR), addressing representative bias involves fostering an environment where clinicians are encouraged to consider a wide range of differential diagnoses based on evidence rather than surface-level similarity alone.
