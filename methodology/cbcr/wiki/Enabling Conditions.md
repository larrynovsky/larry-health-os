# [[Illness Script]] Theory

Custers recently summarized scripts as high-level conceptual knowledge structures in long-term memory, representing general event sequences, interconnected by temporal and often causal relationships. Scripts are activated in appropriate contexts containing relevant variables, including clinical findings. Slots in the reasoning process can be filled with information from the actual situation, retrieved from memory, or inferred from context (Custers [[ch03_12_bias and error in clinical reasoning]]).

**Illness scripts**, first introduced by Barrows and Feltovich, are stored in long-term memory as units containing three components: enabling conditions (past history and causes), fault (pathophysiology), and consequences (signs and symptoms) (Feltovics and Barrows [[ch03_01_concepts and definitions]]). These scripts are further elaborated by Schmidt and Boshuizen (Schmidt and Boshuizen 1993).

Illness scripts are shaped by experience and continually refined throughout one’s clinical practice. When an experienced physician sees a patient, verbal and nonverbal information immediately activates relevant illness scripts. This effortless, fast thinking process is referred to as script activation.

In some cases, only one script is activated leading directly to the correct diagnosis (e.g., type II diabetes mellitus). In other cases, multiple scripts are activated and the most likely diagnosis is chosen through analytic or slow thinking by comparing and contrasting alternative illness scripts. Early learners may not activate any scripts initially, while experts may activate one or several.
