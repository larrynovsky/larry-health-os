In Elstein and colleagues' seminal work on medical problem-solving elstein-et-al-1978, researchers noted that physician performance on one patient or case did not predict performance on a subsequent content area or case, giving rise to the phenomenon of **case specificity**. This finding would be quite surprising if medical problem-solving were a general skill.

A second vexing issue in practice is the more recently highlighted phenomenon of **context specificity** durning-et-al-2011. Context specificity refers to the discovery that a physician can see two patients with the same chief complaint and identical symptoms and physical findings, yet arrive at different diagnoses based on the context. This context can be either helpful in reaching the correct diagnosis (Hobus et al., 1987) or detrimental leading to error (Eva, 2005). In essence, factors beyond the 'essential content' drive a physician's clinical reasoning.

Durning and Artino assert that the outcome of clinical reasoning is influenced by context durning-and-artino-2011. This includes the physician themselves, the patient, the system in which they operate, and their interactions. The notion of **system** encompasses factors such as appointment length, location, support systems, and clinic staffing (Durning and Artino 2011). The importance of situativity is underscored by examples like situated cognition ch03_05_prototyping-and-semantic-qualifiers, which breaks down clinical reasoning into physician, patient, environment, and their interactions. Similarly, **situated learning** emphasizes participation in an activity and identity formation as part of the learning process rather than merely acquiring generalized facts.
