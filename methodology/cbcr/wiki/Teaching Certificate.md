Most faculty members at medical schools and universities are trained to become adequate clinicians or scientists, with only a minority being specifically trained as teachers. This is paradoxical given the increasing sophistication of education from grade school to university level.

**Table 9.3: UMC Utrecht’s Model of Teaching Certificates for Faculty**
| Certificate or Diploma | Target Group |
|------------------------|--------------|
| Student teaching certificate | Optional for senior medical students choosing an elective teaching rotation (ten Cate 2007) |
| Teaching certificate | Required for all faculty members |
| Advanced teaching certificate | Required for senior faculty in leadership roles |
| Postgraduate scholarly educator certificate | Optional for senior medical educators aspiring a career in the scholarship of education |
| PhD diploma in health professions education research | For those educators aiming to become researchers of education in the health professions |

If teaching remained constant over time, experienced teachers could simply learn from their colleagues and past experiences. However, with rapid changes in the educational landscape, new faculty members face different challenges than when they were students themselves.

Medical educators worldwide recognize that faculty should be trained before being allowed to teach, just as medical students cannot treat patients without proper training. While this might seem overly strict in practice, universities now require new faculty to obtain a basic teaching certificate and an advanced certification for those in leadership roles.

**Molenaar et al. (2009)** provide a detailed framework of teaching competencies for medical educators, distinguishing between various domains such as development, organization, execution, coaching, assessment, and program evaluation. This framework also delineates levels of responsibility from leadership to actual teaching, providing an excellent foundation for faculty development programs.
