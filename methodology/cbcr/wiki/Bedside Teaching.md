# Bedside Teaching and Patient Demonstration
Until well into the seventeenth century, academic medicine was almost exclusively theoretical, with reasoning employed to defend theses or construct logical arguments rather than diagnose or treat patients.
In 1543, Batista de Monte introduced bedside teaching in Padua, marking a shift towards empirical clinical reasoning. However, this approach did not gain traction due to its discontinuation and limited access to suitable patients. Herman Boerhaave at Leyden University was more successful but his teachings were orchestrated patient demonstrations rather than sessions focused on clinical reasoning.
Thomas Bond's lectures at the Pennsylvania Hospital in 1766 marked a significant advancement, introducing empirical elements into clinical reasoning. Unlike Boerhaave, Bond made predictions about autopsy findings based on clinical observations and acknowledged errors openly to improve understanding of pathology and treatment options.
## Abraham Flexner and the Science of Clinical Medicine
Abraham Flexner advocated for an integrated approach to teaching clinical medicine through patient observation, instructor demonstrations, and principle exposition. He emphasized the importance of students' active engagement with patients in a scientific manner, akin to laboratory research, but faced resistance due to the impracticality of postponing clinical judgments indefinitely.
In the 1950s and 60s, teaching methods remained haphazard, often based on personal experiences rather than systematic reasoning. This period saw the development of applied technology approaches to diagnosis, including early computer programs designed to simulate clinical problem-solving scenarios ([[ch03_02_early thinking of clinical reasoning the computer analogy]]).
These tools aimed to provide case-specific recommendations and were primarily used for assessment purposes.
Patient management problems (PMPs) emerged as paper-and-pencil-based simulations aiming to replicate real-world clinical situations. Though they offered immediate feedback, their limited flexibility led to their eventual decline in use.
