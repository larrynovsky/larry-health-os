updated: '2026-05-13'

With increasing clinical information stored as illness scripts in the long-term memory of the physician, diagnostic reasoning should steadily become more accurate. However, studies have shown that more novice clinicians (e.g., those just out of training such as recent graduates from residency education) sometimes outperform physicians who have been in practice for some time (e.g., “experts”) on the recall of details from clinical cases seen. This finding was coined by Schmidt and Boshuizen as the intermediate effect ch03_07_encapsulation-of-knowledge-and-the-intermediate-effect. While inexperienced clinicians may consciously use pathophysiological thinking when solving clinical problems, the frequent use of similar thinking pathways leads to efficient shortcuts, and after a while it may no longer be possible to unfold these pathways. The pathophysiological knowledge about the disease becomes encapsulated into diagnostic labels or high-level simplified causal models that explain signs and symptoms [[Illness script]] (Schmidt and Mamede 2015).
