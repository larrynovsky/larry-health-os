In the mid-20th century, Computer-Assisted Instruction (CAI) emerged as an innovative teaching tool for medical education. Following Abraham Flexner's advocacy for a more scientific approach to clinical reasoning, new mathematical and statistical techniques were applied to medical diagnosis. The development of the electronic computer provided opportunities to integrate these methods into clinical problem-solving processes.

In the 1960s, early digital computer programs were developed to instruct students how to solve clinical problems. These systems created an illusion of flexibility using branched programming, where students could ask questions or suggest actions from a predefined vocabulary list, and the machine would provide appropriate responses.

Some advanced CAI programs could generate cases based on specific diagnoses by selecting symptoms and findings statistically ch02_05_abraham-flexner-and-the-science-of-clinical-medicine. However, these early systems were limited in scope to providing case-specific recommendations, focusing primarily on diagnostic drills rather than teaching an explicit general method applicable across different cases.

Conceptually similar but paper-based approaches like Patient Management Problems (PMPs) also emerged. These tools use branched programming to simulate clinical situations and provide feedback on chosen actions ch02_05_abraham-flexner-and-the-science-of-clinical-medicine. Despite their initial promise, PMPs became less popular due to limitations in allowing legitimate flexibility in problem-solving approaches.

While these early CAI systems were experimental tools rather than practical teaching aids due to the technological constraints of the time, they laid the groundwork for future developments in medical education technology.
