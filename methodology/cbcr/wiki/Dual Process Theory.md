## Dual Process Theory

Dual process theory refers to two distinct cognitive processes involved in reasoning ch03_08_system-1-and-2-thinking-as-dual-processes. Briefly, dual process theory posits that individuals engage in both fast thinking (System 1) and slow thinking (System 2). Fast thinking is characterized by being quick, subconscious, and effortless. Examples include pattern recognition ch03_08_system-1-and-2-thinking-as-dual-processes, where a physician immediately identifies the cardinal features of Graves’ disease upon observing symptoms such as exophthalmia, fine resting tremor, and thyromegaly.

Slow or analytic thinking (System 2) is effortful and conscious. It involves deliberate reasoning processes like calculating an acid-base status for a patient ch03_13_neuroscience-and-visual-expertise-in-clinical-reasoning. Daniel Kahneman’s book *Thinking, Fast and Slow* popularized dual process theory ch03_08_system-1-and-2-thinking-as-dual-processes. Recent research suggests that both processes are used concurrently in practice, reflecting a continuum between fast and slow thinking rather than an either-or dichotomy ch03_08_system-1-and-2-thinking-as-dual-processes.

Efficient clinical work necessitates the use of fast thinking to handle routine tasks without overloading working memory. Analytic reasoning is reserved for complex decisions where effortful, conscious thought is required.
