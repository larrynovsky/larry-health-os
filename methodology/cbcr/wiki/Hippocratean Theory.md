updated: '2026-05-13'

Until well into the seventeenth century, academic medicine was almost exclusively theoretical. Reasoning played an important role but was used primarily to defend theses or construct logical arguments rather than arrive at diagnoses or select therapies [[ch02_02_clinical reasoning in the hippocratean era]]. The introduction of bedside teaching by Batista de Monte in Padua in 1543 marked a potential shift towards more empirical clinical reasoning, though little is known about his actual practice and it was soon discontinued. Attempts to introduce this method in the Netherlands, notably by Willem van der Straaten in Utrecht (1636) and Jan Van Heurne in Leyden (1638), also failed.

Herman Boerhaave at Leiden University succeeded better but even his bedside teachings were largely demonstrations rather than teaching sessions focused on clinical reasoning [[ch02_02_clinical reasoning in the hippocratean era]]. His goal was to integrate theoretical knowledge from books and lectures with clinical experience, emphasizing advanced Hippocratean and Galenic theory.

A significant advancement came in 1766 when Dr. Thomas Bond at the Medical College of the University of Pennsylvania delivered his Introductory Lecture to a Course of Clinical Observations. Bond had unrestricted access to patients and introduced empirical elements into clinical reasoning [[ch02_02_clinical reasoning in the hippocratean era]]. Unlike Boerhaave, who merely demonstrated findings at autopsy, Bond predicted these outcomes. Recognizing potential errors in his predictions, he acknowledged mistakes openly and proposed alternative methods for better treatment. This approach opened doors to improving clinical reasoning and understanding the link between visible pathology and disease.
