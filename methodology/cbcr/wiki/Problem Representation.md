Once students have started to learn the vocabulary physicians use to describe patients' clinical concerns, they will be ready to begin using these words to formulate the **clinical problem** that the case requires them to solve. This formulation is called the **problem representation** [[ch03_01_concepts and definitions]]. A problem representation combines situational information about the patient with the clinician's knowledge to create a structured and actionable description of the problem (Feltovich and Barrows 1984; Gruppen and Frohna 2002).

Constructing a problem representation involves transforming specific symptoms and signs into a conceptualization using **semantic qualifiers** [[ch03_05_prototyping and semantic qualifiers]]. At this stage, the words reflect meaning assigned to case features in relation to temporal and potential causal relationships (Auclair 2007). Students move from knowing specific case feature descriptions to assigning meaningful relationships among them—from remembering to understanding.

In clinical reasoning, constructing a problem representation occurs between data acquisition and hypothesis generation (Chang et al. 1998). Abstract semantic qualifiers help build an initial conceptualization of the problem before generating diagnostic hypotheses (Nendaz and Bordage 2002). Problem representations then activate medical knowledge from long-term memory to generate plausible diagnoses for the specific case under consideration. Clinicians purposefully direct further data gathering in relation to these hypotheses.

Generating a problem representation is often an unconscious process (Bowen 2006). Teachers can make this step explicit by asking students, “What problem are we trying to solve?” Feedback on students' representations should promote appropriate abstraction of case features using semantic qualifiers and identifying key attributes—onset, site, severity, chronology, and context—when describing the clinical problem based on the chief complaint and history.

For example, Alicia’s problem representation could be: a middle-aged female with a chronic, gradually progressive symmetrical oligoarticular process involving small joints characterized by moderate to severe morning stiffness. Robert's problem representation might be: young male with sudden onset of brief, self-limited syncope in the setting of chronic, progressive dyspnea and episodic chest tightness that resolves with rest.

In complex cases, more than one problem representation may be possible, emphasizing different key attributes to trigger a broader set of plausible diagnostic hypotheses. Teachers should encourage students to generate appropriate representations addressing all relevant case features using the right semantic qualifiers.

Students often want their representations to be 'right or wrong.' It is crucial to emphasize that construction of a problem representation helps clinicians consider plausible diagnoses early in data gathering. Problem representations are not binary; they improve with more thorough and relevant detail using proper semantic qualifiers for the specific clinical problem.

At the end of case presentations, students often summarize their patient’s problem in one sentence, known as summary statements or assessment statements. These serve different purposes from problem representations—summaries synthesize specific characteristics for a patient's problem and set up discussions about management or testing. Problem representation is more generic and occurs early in data gathering, while summaries are final reflections after gathering further data to narrow plausible differential diagnoses.
