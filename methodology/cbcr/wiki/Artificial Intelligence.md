# Artificial Intelligence
Artificial intelligence (AI) refers to computer programs characterized by flexibility and adaptivity, which can accommodate a broad range of user input and respond similarly broadly, including providing feedback or recommendations ch02_06_artificial-intelligence-and-problem-based-learning. In complex domains like medicine, AI systems are termed expert systems or intelligent tutoring systems (ITS) in educational contexts. These systems employ chains of simple operations applied to simple content to manage complexity.

In clinical medicine, automated diagnostic systems distinguish between disease knowledge and diagnostic procedures ch02_06_artificial-intelligence-and-problem-based-learning. Early examples include INTERNIST and MYCIN ch02_06_artificial-intelligence-and-problem-based-learning, while GUIDON-MANAGE was developed specifically to introduce medical students to diagnostic reasoning processes.

AI draws on principles of human problem-solving, which were originally derived from early programmable machines. This approach appeared promising for education, as it suggested that clinical problems could be taught independently of specific content knowledge ch02_06_artificial-intelligence-and-problem-based-learning. However, research by Elstein et al. (1978) revealed significant differences in expertise between novices and experts in generating hypotheses during diagnosis, indicating superior performance is a result of experience rather than general problem-solving skills ch02_06_artificial-intelligence-and-problem-based-learning, ch03_02_early-thinking-of-clinical-reasoning-the-computer-analogy.

Clinical educators now distinguish between pattern recognition and hypothesis generation approaches to solving diagnostic problems. While pattern recognition can be highly effective with extensive experience, it is not easily taught ch02_06_artificial-intelligence-and-problem-based-learning. Alternative methods have been proposed for clinical reasoning education, such as the Clinical Problem Analysis (CPA) approach which emphasizes constructing differential diagnoses and avoids premature closure ch02_06_artificial-intelligence-and-problem-based-learning.

Teaching clinical reasoning effectively involves a case-based format, focusing on small steps that encourage critical thinking. It is important to avoid emphasizing correct diagnosis outcomes to prevent undesirable behavior like guessing or jumping to conclusions ch02_06_artificial-intelligence-and-problem-based-learning. The goal should be to teach students how to formulate comprehensive differential diagnoses and manage uncertainty effectively ch02_06_artificial-intelligence-and-problem-based-learning.

Early thinking about clinical reasoning often drew analogies with computer systems, assuming algorithmic processes in working memory. However, current evidence suggests that while computers can provide useful support through clinical decision support systems (CDSS), they cannot yet match the intuitive diagnostic skills of experienced physicians ch03_02_early-thinking-of-clinical-reasoning-the-computer-analogy.

Overall, teaching clinical reasoning should focus on developing systematic methods for managing uncertainty and formulating differential diagnoses in a case-based format. This approach is best suited to junior medical students' learning needs.
