Once students have gained a certain level of fluency with clinical vocabulary and learned to conceptualize patients’ problems using semantic qualifiers, teachers can introduce the concept of illness scripts (Custers 2015) [[illness script]]. Illness scripts are mental representations used in clinical reasoning that develop over time with clinical experience. They consist of high-level, precompiled knowledge structures stored in long-term memory and represent general event sequences interconnected by temporal, causal, or hierarchical relationships. These scripts can be activated as whole units based on context and contain variables and slots for new information [[illness script]].

Most medical students early in their education do not have enough clinical experience to fully develop illness scripts but will start forming rudimentary versions as they learn about typical presentations of clinical cases. For example, a student with personal experience may possess an illness script for “flu” or “motion sickness.” When provided with a structured schema that elicits components of illness scripts, students can organize information into these structures (Table 4.3 shows the progression from initial problem representation to summary statement in diagnostic reasoning).

To aid this process, Table 4.4 provides an illness script worksheet that categorizes the components—enabling conditions, pathophysiological fault, and clinical consequences—with space for students to record typical findings concisely. This includes attributes such as age, sex, race, ethnicity, family history, genetics, environmental exposures, habits, medications, nested comorbidities, pathophysiological mechanisms, symptom onset, site, severity, chronology, physical exam findings, laboratory results, and imaging findings.

Students must gradually build a mental repository of illness scripts in their long-term memory that become readily available for comparison at new patient encounters. This requires extensive case elaboration with guidance, both real or simulated patient interactions, to stimulate study and reflection operation log [[ch09_02_a brief introduction to curriculum development]].

During a CBCR session (lasting 1′45″–2′30″ h), peer teachers guide the discussion using additional information provided in the peer teacher version of the case. They lead by reading initial patient vignettes and facilitating questions, inventorying answers from students, reflecting on responses with mini-lectures to provide background or diagnostic test information ch10_07_tasks-of-peer-teachers, and summarizing the case at the end ([[ch06_05_a cbcr session]]). Peer teachers must ensure every student participates actively and avoid giving advance information that might affect the natural progression of the case.

Mini-lectures during CBCR sessions aim to provide directly applicable background information or deepen understanding of topics. They should be concise, using minimal text on slides and incorporating images for clarity [[ch10_08_the mini lecture]].
