# Course Development for CBCR
Introducing [[Case-Based Clinical Reasoning]] (CBCR) into an existing medical curriculum can be straightforward and does not require extensive organizational changes or a long development timeline. A well-planned introduction of a CBCR course, following the format presented in this book, aligns with principles of modern medical education such as being student-centered, problem-based, integrated, community-based, elective, and systematic (SPICES). Implementing a new CBCR course should be planned at least one year ahead to allow for case writing and other preparatory activities.

## Steps for Course Development
1. **[[Educational Needs Assessment]]**: Assess the need for introducing CBCR through structured interviews or questionnaires with stakeholders such as clinicians, basic scientists, and students. This helps secure support from decision-making bodies (committees, deans) to proceed with course development.
2. **Content Needs Assessment & Objectives**: Determine which pathologies are prioritized for case discussions based on curriculum year and relevant prior knowledge of students. Sources like health statistics can inform the selection of cases that reflect common medical conditions.
3. **Intended Learners**: Decide who will take the course, whether it is mandatory or elective, and when it should be scheduled.
4. **General Course Framework**: Develop a blueprint outlining objectives, case titles, session details, clinical disciplines involved, number of cases per discipline, origin of cases, group sizes, scheduling, physical requirements, and teacher numbers.
5. **Method of Assessment & Examination Rules**: Define the course credits, criteria for satisfactory participation, and testing methods to evaluate knowledge and skills acquired through CBCR.
6. **Coordination & Teachers**: Establish a course director or coordinator with consultants from various clinical departments. Consultants should be assigned to groups throughout the full course duration, even outside their specialties.
7. **Funding**: Estimate funding requirements for case development, annual updates, coordination efforts, consultant time, exam preparation/administration, and administration of student data/materials/evaluation. Junior doctors can act as consultants if provided with high-quality cases and proper guidance to reduce costs significantly.
8. **Program Evaluation**: Develop a system for continuous improvement through feedback from both students and teachers regarding case quality, teaching skills, and facilities.

## A Brief Introduction to [[Curriculum Development]]
A curriculum is defined as "a planned educational experience" (Thomas et al. 2016) applied at macro-, meso-, and micro-levels. Janet Grant proposed that a curriculum includes intended aims, objectives, content, experiences, outcomes, processes, training structure, methods of learning, teaching, feedback, supervision, and organizational management. For major curriculum innovation projects, Mulder and ten Cate (2006) constructed a ten-element definition.

A full curriculum description should include a mission statement, educational philosophy, intended learners, general framework, course/unit descriptions, assessment methods, organizational structure, staffing conditions, finances, facilities, and quality assurance. While this is extensive, it remains a living thing effective only as delivered by teachers and received by students. Authors distinguish between the planned curriculum, delivered curriculum, experienced curriculum, and hidden curriculum (unwritten rules conveyed implicitly).

It's important to be aware of these distinctions while ensuring that pathways followed by students lead effectively toward common goals in medical education. The success of a curriculum varies depending on student motivation and local/national context.
