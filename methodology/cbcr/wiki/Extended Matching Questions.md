Educators often use extended matching questions (EMQs) as a method to assess clinical reasoning, particularly suitable for written or electronic testing formats. EMQs feature a central theme, such as symptoms or diagnostic challenges, accompanied by a list of options like diagnoses or lab results. A lead question prompts the test-taker to select from this extensive list based on case vignettes provided thereafter.

For example, an EMQ might present a theme such as 'fatigue' and ask: “What is the most likely diagnosis?” with multiple patient scenarios following to provide context for each selection. This format reduces the likelihood of guessing due to the large number of options (typically 10–20), making it more challenging for test-takers who may not have a deep understanding of clinical reasoning.

EMQs are widely used in standardized tests like those administered by the National Board of Medical Examiners in the United States. However, their application outside this context is less common due to the considerable number of questions and testing time required to ensure reliability—often up to 100 items and 4 hours.

Despite these requirements, EMQs have been praised for their effectiveness in assessing key features critical to clinical problem-solving. They align well with the **Key-Feature Approach** ch03_05_prototyping-and-semantic-qualifiers, which focuses on essential aspects of patient management that learners often find challenging or crucial.

Technical limitations currently restrict EMQs from evaluating interdependent reasoning chains and differential diagnosis rankings, though sophisticated software might address these issues in the future. In contrast to methods like the **[[Script Concordance Test]]** ch07_02_alignment-with-actual-cases-discussed, which provide pre-defined hypotheses rather than requiring test-takers to generate their own diagnoses, EMQs offer a more nuanced assessment of clinical reasoning skills.
