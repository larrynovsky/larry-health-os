Clinical reasoning has roots as old as humanity, but Hippocrates (±460 BC – ±370 BC) is credited with emphasizing the natural causes of diseases over divine explanations. He proposed that diseases result from imbalances in the four humors: yellow bile, black bile, blood, and phlegm, an idea expanded upon by Galen (131–216). Hippocrates’ contributions to clinical reasoning included a focus on meticulous observation and documentation of symptoms, bodily fluids, excretions, environment, diet, and living habits. He summarized practical knowledge in the form of aphorisms, which were often heuristic rules like “if symptom X, then always consider disease Y.” These aphorisms served as early examples of clinical reasoning based on empirical experience rather than theoretical deduction from humoral theory. In contemporary practice, such heuristics remain important for diagnosis, though they are now informed by a deeper understanding of biomedical and pathophysiological mechanisms [[ch03_12_bias and error in clinical reasoning]].
