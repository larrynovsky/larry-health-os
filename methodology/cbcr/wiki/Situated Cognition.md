Situated cognition is a theory that posits understanding arises from the interaction between individuals and their environment. In the context of medical education, situated cognition can help explain phenomena such as **case specificity** ch03_09_case-specificity-and-context-specificity and **context specificity** ch03_09_case-specificity-and-context-specificity. Case specificity refers to the finding that a physician's performance on one case does not predict their performance on another, indicating that medical problem-solving is highly specific to individual cases rather than being a generalizable skill. Context specificity highlights how similar symptoms and chief complaints can lead to different diagnoses depending on the context in which they occur (e.g., **dual-process theory** ch03_02_early-thinking-of-clinical-reasoning-the-computer-analogy). The context, including the patient, physician, system interactions, and environmental factors such as appointment location and clinic staffing, significantly influences clinical reasoning. Situated cognition suggests that clinical reasoning emerges from these interdependent components rather than isolated knowledge acquisition (ch03_12_bias-and-error-in-clinical-reasoning, ch08_11_stage-iv-the-results-of-diagnostic-tests-are-provided).
