# Neuroscience and Visual Expertise in [[Clinical Reasoning]]
While neuroscience is quickly uncovering many cognitive processes, clinical reasoning has hardly been subject to such studies. However, a new line of research has emerged recently that aims to explore the biological underpinnings of clinical reasoning. Indeed, one of the challenges in clinical reasoning is its lack of introspection or visualization, making techniques like functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and electroencephalogram (EEG) particularly promising for enhancing our understanding of System 1 thinking [[ch03_02_early thinking of clinical reasoning the computer analogy]].

One of the first publications in this domain is from Durning et al., who studied brain processes with fMRI techniques in novices and experts solving clinical problems through vignette-based multiple choice questions. The study observed activity across various regions of the prefrontal cortex (Durning et al. 2015). Although preliminary, fMRI shows promise for future investigations into clinical reasoning.

A related area of investigation is visual expertise (Bezemer 2017; van der Gijp et al. 2016). Medicine is a highly visual profession, not only in specific disciplines such as radiology, pathology, dermatology, surgery, and cardiology but also in primary care (Kok and Jarodzka 2017). Visually observing patients or their representations can lead to immediate System 1 recognition.

In sum, this chapter provides an overview of theoretical concepts, frequently used terms, and significant thinkers in the field of clinical reasoning. While much of the cited literature appeared after the initial model of case-based clinical reasoning (CBCR) was developed in 1992 (ten Cate 1994), none of these recommendations conflict with the CBCR approach.

Although there are still many gaps in our understanding of clinical reasoning, progress towards a more thorough comprehension continues to advance.
