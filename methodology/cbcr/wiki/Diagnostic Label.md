In medical education and practice, a **diagnostic label** is used to encapsulate complex pathophysiological knowledge into simplified models that explain signs and symptoms of diseases. This process involves the formation of illness scripts in long-term memory, making diagnostic reasoning more efficient but sometimes less detailed as clinicians gain experience.

The concept of the intermediate effect illustrates this phenomenon: while novice clinicians may perform better than experienced ones in recalling specific details from clinical cases, they tend to use pathophysiological thinking which becomes encapsulated over time into high-level simplified causal models (Schmidt and Boshuizen 1993). This transition can hinder the ability to unfold detailed reasoning pathways but enhances diagnostic accuracy.

**Contrastive learning** is a teaching strategy that aims to enhance this process by prompting students to compare and contrast different clinical cases, such as distinguishing between rheumatoid arthritis and osteoarthritis. By focusing on critical features that differentiate these conditions, learners can better understand the nuances of each illness script and develop stronger diagnostic skills.

This approach is crucial in preparing early medical students for clinical reasoning by introducing them to differential diagnoses and the process of comparing plausible diagnostic hypotheses (Ark et al. 2007). For example, when teaching about rheumatoid arthritis versus osteoarthritis, teachers might highlight specific enabling conditions, pathophysiological faults, and clinical consequences that differentiate these diseases.

By employing contrastive learning techniques, medical educators can help students efficiently encapsulate knowledge while ensuring they retain the ability to recognize subtle differences between similar clinical presentations. This balance is essential for developing effective diagnostic skills in novice clinicians (Schmidt and Mamede 2015).
