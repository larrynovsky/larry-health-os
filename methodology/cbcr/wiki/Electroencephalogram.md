An electroencephalogram (EEG) is a tool used to record electrical activity in the brain and has shown promise in studying the biological underpinnings of System 1 thinking, which is crucial for clinical reasoning. Recent research ch03_12_bias-and-error-in-clinical-reasoning using EEG has provided insights into how medical experts process visual information, such as recognizing abnormalities in patients or images, instantly leading to intuitive diagnoses (Bezemer 2017; van der Gijp et al. 2016). This research is part of a broader trend in neuroscience that aims to understand the cognitive processes involved in clinical reasoning better.

EEG complements other neuroimaging techniques like functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) ch03_09_case-specificity-and-context-specificity, offering high temporal resolution and real-time data, making it especially useful for studying rapid decision-making processes in medicine. While preliminary studies suggest that EEG could be a valuable tool for future investigations into clinical reasoning, further research is needed to validate its potential fully.

The application of EEG to clinical reasoning highlights the importance of visual expertise in diagnosing conditions quickly and accurately (Kok and Jarodzka 2017). Visual observations can trigger immediate recognition without the need for verbal processing, a key aspect of System 1 thinking ch03_08_system-1-and-2-thinking-as-dual-processes. This makes EEG an interesting area of study in enhancing our understanding of how medical professionals use their visual skills to make clinical judgments.
