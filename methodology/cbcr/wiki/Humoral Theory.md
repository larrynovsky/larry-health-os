Hippocrates, around the 5th century BC, introduced a naturalistic approach to disease causation, marking a departure from previous beliefs that attributed illnesses to divine causes. He proposed that diseases arise from imbalances in the four humors: yellow bile, black bile, blood, and phlegm ch02_02_clinical-reasoning-in-the-hippocratean-era. This theory was further developed by Galen (131–216 AD) and remained influential for two millennia despite lacking empirical evidence. Hippocrates' emphasis on careful observation of symptoms, bodily fluids, diet, and environmental factors laid the groundwork for systematic clinical reasoning ch02_02_clinical-reasoning-in-the-hippocratean-era. His aphorisms, often in 'if... then' format, served as early heuristics guiding diagnostic processes. While these rules were based on experience rather than a comprehensive theoretical framework like the humoral theory, they influenced modern clinical reasoning practices by providing practical guidelines ch02_02_clinical-reasoning-in-the-hippocratean-era.
