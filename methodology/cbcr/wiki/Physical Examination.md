In traditional medical education, preclinical medical students typically learn the components of the physical examination and then assemble these into a logical sequence for conducting head-to-toe examinations on any patient ch04_06_hypothesis-driven-inquiry. However, this approach to learning the physical examination does not effectively promote its role as a data-gathering step in the clinical reasoning process. Without context, students may struggle to comprehend the significance of abnormal findings and how they contribute to differential diagnoses.

Yudkowsky et al. suggest an alternative approach that emphasizes contextual learning ch04_06_hypothesis-driven-inquiry. This method embeds instruction on physical examination maneuvers within diagnostic reasoning tasks. It focuses on helping students anticipate which examination maneuvers will help discriminate between different diagnostic considerations and recognize diagnostically useful findings.

Hasnain et al. also studied the relationship between history-taking behaviors, semantic versus symptom-driven discourse, and diagnostic accuracy ch04_06_hypothesis-driven-inquiry. They identified four interviewing behaviors associated with high diagnostic accuracy: thorough exploration of the chief complaint early in the encounter, asking questions that illustrate a line of reasoning about a diagnostic hypothesis, requesting further clarifying information from patients, and summarizing gathered information. These practices are described as 'purposeful or hypothesis-driven inquiry.'

A guide for implementing this contextual learning approach is available ch04_06_hypothesis-driven-inquiry.
