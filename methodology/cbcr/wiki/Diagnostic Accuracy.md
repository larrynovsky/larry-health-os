Georges Bordage introduced the concept of **semantic qualifiers** referring to abstract terms used to organize patient information ch03_12_bias-and-error-in-clinical-reasoning. These qualifiers, such as 'acute', 'monoarticular,' and 'arthritis' in a case involving knee swelling and pain, help clinicians recognize patterns that are integral to their illness scripts. Semantic qualifiers are crucial for diagnostic accuracy because they allow the clinician's memory to contain prototypes of diseases ch04_05_contrastive-learning. These mental representations enable recognition by translating observed symptoms into clinically meaningful terminology.

Diagnostic accuracy also hinges on overcoming common biases in clinical reasoning, such as **availability bias**, where diagnoses are influenced by what is easily recalled; **representative bias**, where signs and symptoms overshadow prevalence data; **confirmation bias**, focusing too narrowly on confirming initial suspicions; **anchoring bias**, failing to adjust the diagnosis with new data; **bounded rationality bias**, prematurely concluding the diagnostic process after an anticipated diagnosis; and **outcome bias**, judging decisions based on outcomes rather than logic ch03_12_bias-and-error-in-clinical-reasoning.

The use of semantic qualifiers and hypothesis-driven inquiry, where clinicians explore the chief complaint thoroughly early in a patient encounter to gather clarifying information, enhances diagnostic accuracy. This approach supports contextual learning by embedding physical examination maneuvers within diagnostic reasoning tasks ch04_06_hypothesis-driven-inquiry. Contrastive learning further aids students by comparing and contrasting similar cases to understand critical features that discriminate between diagnoses ch04_05_contrastive-learning.

The **[[CBCR Test]]** aims to evaluate clinical reasoning but has limitations. Unlike traditional tests, it does not provide interdependent reasoning questions where subsequent answers depend on previous ones or allow for differential diagnosis weighing within answers ch07_06_what-the-utrecht-cbcr-test-does-not-provide. The Utrecht CBCR test, however, compensates by using parallel scenarios with branching options [[ch08_11_stage iv the results of diagnostic tests are provided]].

In conclusion, diagnostic accuracy is improved through semantic qualifiers, overcoming reasoning biases, hypothesis-driven inquiry, and contrastive learning methods. These strategies help students develop robust illness scripts that facilitate recognition in new patient encounters.
