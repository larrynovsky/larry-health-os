# Diagnostic Systems
Artificial intelligence (AI) diagnostic systems are computer programs characterized by flexibility and adaptivity, capable of accommodating a broad range of user input and responding with a similarly wide array of feedback and recommendations. When applied to complex domains like medicine, these AI programs are known as **expert systems** or **intelligent tutoring systems (ITS)** in education. They rely on chains of simple operations applied to large amounts of simple content to create complex procedures and knowledge.
In clinical medicine, diagnostic systems feature a strict distinction between disease knowledge and diagnostic procedures. Prominent examples from the 1980s include INTERNIST [[ch03_02_early thinking of clinical reasoning the computer analogy]], MYCIN, and GUIDON-MANAGE, developed to introduce medical students to diagnostic reasoning.
These systems draw heavily on principles of human problem solving, which were influenced by early programmable machines. The approach seemed promising for teaching clinical diagnosis directly, but was challenged by Elstein et al. (1978) who found that experts generate better hypotheses earlier in the process due to experience, not direct instruction. This suggests that exposing students to a broad range of clinical problems is crucial for learning diagnostic reasoning.
Today, researchers distinguish between pattern recognition and hypothesis generation and testing. The latter involves constructing differential diagnoses, though definitions vary [[ch03_01_concepts and definitions]]. Methods like Eddy and Clanton's approach or CPA (Clinical Problem Analysis) aim to systematically construct these diagnoses by clustering findings and considering all possible causes.
Teaching clinical reasoning effectively requires a case-based format. Teachers should avoid emphasizing outcomes over processes, encourage small steps, ask hypothetical questions, and clarify objectives before sessions [[ch01_03_essential features of cbcr education]]. Active participation is crucial, and the best approach involves guided small group sessions with clear communication and active note-taking.
In summary, teaching diagnostic reasoning through systematic case-based methods will be most effective for junior medical students. Operation Log, [[ch01_04_indications for the effectiveness of the cbcr method]]
