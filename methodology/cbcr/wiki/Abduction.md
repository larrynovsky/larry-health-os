Abduction, a term coined by logician C.S. Peirce in the nineteenth century, refers to backward reasoning aimed at explaining why a given observation occurs. In clinical reasoning, abduction is critical for forming hypotheses based on surprising or unexpected observations. For instance, if an adult male patient presents with wet grass, one might hypothesize that it has rained recently ([[ch03_12_bias and error in clinical reasoning]]). However, this hypothesis must be tested against alternative explanations (e.g., a neighbor’s sprinkler). Abductive reasoning is essential for acquiring new ideas in clinical practice because it allows clinicians to generate hypotheses from surprising observations, which are then tested through further investigation. This process underscores the importance of considering possible causations and testing initial assumptions, thereby driving forward the diagnostic process ([[ch03_09_case specificity and context specificity]]).
