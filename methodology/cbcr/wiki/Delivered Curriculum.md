“Curriculum,” often defined as “a planned educational experience”thomas-et-al-2016, has evolved to encompass various levels of education: macro (government-defined requirements), meso (institutional rules and methods), and micro (lesson planning). Janet Grant proposed a comprehensive curriculum definition, stating it is “a statement of the intended aims and objectives, content, experiences, outcomes, and processes of an educational program, including descriptions of training structure, learning methods, teaching, feedback, and supervision”grant-2010. Mulder and ten Cate further outlined a practical ten-element framework for curriculum development based on extensive experiencemulder-and-ten-cate-2006.

A full curriculum includes a mission statement, objectives, learner descriptions, educational philosophy, general curriculum framework, unit/course descriptions, assessment methods with rules on student progress and exams, organizational structure, personnel conditions, finances, facilities, and quality assurance. While these elements align with international medical curricula standardslindgren-2012, the effectiveness of a curriculum relies heavily on its delivery by educators and reception by students.

Authors have identified four types of curricula: planned (as described), delivered (teachers’ understanding and execution), experienced (students’ perception), and hidden (unwritten rules conveyed implicitly). These distinctions highlight that pathways to educational goals can vary widely. Each curriculum must be tailored to the specific context, including student motivation and national/local conditions. Students motivated to become doctors may pursue any path deemed appropriate for their degree, regardless of planned curricula or jurisdictionswijnen-meijer-et-al-2013. This intrinsic motivation should be valued and encouraged as long as it supports constructive career development.

While there is no single “best” curriculum, success depends on students who follow it and the local context. Variations in pathways to educational goals should not be avoided but recognized and managed carefully to ensure effective learning towards common medical education objectives.
