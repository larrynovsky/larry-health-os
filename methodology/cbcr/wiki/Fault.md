# [[Illness Script]] Theory
Custers recently summarized scripts as high-level conceptual knowledge structures in long-term memory, representing general event sequences, interconnected by temporal and often causal or hierarchical relationships ("usually diabetes type II occurs at older age, obesity is associated; late symptoms might include vascular problems in the retina, lower limbs and other places"). Scripts are activated as integral wholes in appropriate contexts that should contain relevant variables, including clinical findings from the patient. “Slots” in the reasoning process can be filled with information present in the actual situation, retrieved from memory, or inferred from the context wikilinks(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Custers_2015). Illness scripts, first introduced by Barrows and Feltovich, are believed to be chunks in long-term memory containing three components: enabling conditions (past history and causes), fault (pathophysiology), and consequences (signs and symptoms) wikilinks(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feltovics_and_Barrows_1984). These scripts are further elaborated by Schmidt and Boshuizen in 1993. Illness scripts are stored in long-term memory as units with temporal (i.e., sequential) components, akin to a film script of unfolding events; patients are remembered as instances of these scripts.

With experience, physicians develop a larger repertoire of illness scripts that become more elaborate over time wikilinks(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Script_activation). Illness scripts are shaped by experience and continually refined throughout one’s clinical practice. When an experienced physician initially sees a patient, their verbal and nonverbal information immediately activates relevant illness scripts. This effortless, fast thinking is referred to as script activation wikilinks(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Script_activation). In some cases, only one script is activated leading directly to the correct diagnosis (e.g., "type II diabetes mellitus"). In other instances, multiple scripts are activated, and then theory suggests we choose the most likely diagnosis by comparing and contrasting alternative illness scripts that were activated through analytic or slow thinking. Early learners may not activate any scripts when they first see a patient, while experts might activate one or several.
